A savings fund for first-time buyers

Save for your first Dutch home in a fund that steps down its risk as buying day gets closer.

You invest every month for 8 to 10 years. Early on the money grows in global shares. Then it moves step by step into buffered shares and safer assets, so a crash just before you buy takes less of your deposit with it.

Every number on this page is simulated from euro-area and Dutch data (1999–2026), repeated across 40 re-calibrations of the model so you see the uncertainty, not one lucky run.

How we chose it

Buffered shares

The first 10% of a fall is absorbed

In the middle years your shares are held with a buffer: alongside the index, the fund buys a put option at today's level and sells one 10% lower. If the market falls up to 10% over the year, the buffer pays for the fall. Below that, you take the losses beyond 10%.

The protection has a price, paid out of the gains. The fund holds four slices that each renew once a year, three months apart, so it never resets all at once.

How we calculate this

Method, limits and sources

Method

  • The economy moves between four regimes (expansion, late cycle, recession, recovery) as a Markov chain. Shares, Dutch house prices, interest rates, mortgage rates and inflation follow regime-dependent models fitted to 1999–2026 data.
  • Each run simulates 400 futures, month by month, for every strategy on the same futures, so differences come from the strategy and not from luck.
  • Parameter uncertainty: the history is resampled in blocks (stationary bootstrap) and the model re-fitted 200 times. The page uses 40 of these; the ranges show how much the answer depends on which history we happened to get.
  • The buffer is priced with Black–Scholes using implied volatility linked to the regime, with a volatility premium and skew measured from VIX and SKEW data.
  • Dutch 2026 rules: income test (financieringslast table, test rate), 100% loan-to-value, starters' exemption from transfer tax, NHG, box 3 tax on the pot each year.
  • This page runs the same model as the Python version in your browser. An automated test feeds both the same random numbers and checks they agree to 1 part in a billion.

Limits

  • History since 1999 holds about two credit cycles. Events outside it (1970s inflation, the Dutch 1978–85 house-price fall) can't appear in the simulations.
  • The two ways of labelling regimes disagree on most months. Switch on HMM regimes to see how much the answer moves.
  • House prices are the national CBS index. First-time buyers often buy where prices move differently.
  • Income grows steadily with inflation plus 1% a year; job loss and pay shocks are not modelled.
  • Option prices use US volatility data as a proxy for European markets.
  • Box 3 tax uses the deemed-return system without the option to be taxed on actual returns, so tax is an upper bound.
